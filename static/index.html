How are articles vetted and scored?

Quality checks: every article shown passes all of these

Works for any field: medicine, engineering, computer science, transportation, social sciences, physical sciences and more.

  • Peer-reviewed research. Journal articles, reviews and papers in rated conference proceedings only. No preprints, editorials, letters, comments, news or study protocols.
  • Expert-vetted venue. The journal or conference must be on at least one list curated by expert committees that review editorial and peer-review standards: MEDLINE (U.S. National Library of Medicine), CWTS Core (Leiden University), the Norwegian Register (levels 1–2) or the Finnish Publication Forum (JUFO 1–3).
  • Not retracted and no published expression of concern (checked in OpenAlex, Europe PMC and PubMed).
  • Verifiable. Has a DOI and a real abstract that can be cross-checked in OpenAlex.

Estimated risk of bias

A 0–100% estimate (lower is better) combining five factors from the abstract, OpenAlex and PubMed records. It adapts the domains of formal appraisal tools such as Cochrane's Risk of Bias tool so they apply to every field:

  • Study design & safeguards (30%): meta-analyses, systematic reviews and randomized experiments score best; quasi-experiments, controlled lab experiments, benchmark evaluations, validated simulations and cohort studies sit in the middle; surveys, case studies, narrative reviews and case reports score worst. Blinding, multiple sites, adjusting for confounders, validation against real-world data and robustness checks lower the risk; pilot, single-site and unblinded studies raise it.
  • Conflicts of interest (25%): reads the authors' disclosure statement where PubMed has one. Consulting fees, employment, stock or patents with companies, industry funding or company-affiliated authors raise the risk; a clean disclosure with public funding lowers it.
  • Sample size (15%): larger samples, more pooled studies or more benchmark datasets are less prone to chance findings. Not applied to theory, simulations or narrative reviews.
  • Reporting quality (20%): promotional wording ("breakthrough", "revolutionary") and causal claims from studies that can't show cause and effect are signs of spin; acknowledging limitations and reporting statistical uncertainty are good signs.
  • Transparency (10%): pre-registered protocols score best, then openly shared code or data, then free-to-read full text.

AI review (optional)

Where enabled, the "Get AI review" button asks Claude to appraise the abstract domain by domain (selection, confounding, measurement, missing data, selective reporting, conflicts of interest and spin) and give its own risk-of-bias estimate with reasons. It only sees the abstract, so it reports its confidence. Like any AI, it can be wrong.

This is an automated screening aid, not a substitute for reading the paper. Always check the full methods before relying on a source.
